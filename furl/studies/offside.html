An offside space for unfinished things

Keep a piece within reach when it leaves the program. A parked row or term is something you can bring back; a palette template is something you can use repeatedly.

This is a standalone interaction sketch using ordinary text fields. It does not run Hazel, evaluate code, or preserve lexical bindings. The live editor does.

Try the same pieces in three arrangements

Drag a row by its name, or its entire expression by the expression text. Park it on the right; bring a term back onto an expression, or a row back into the main patch. A palette item always makes a copy. Double-click text to edit it.

Parked pieces

You can also click a piece to pick it up, then click a destination. Escape cancels.

Drop one parked row card onto another to join them. The arrows reorder cards. A term card stays independent. One action is one Undo, including joining and editing.

01 · Rail

A stable main program and one ordered, scrollable line off to its side. This is my starting recommendation: easy to put things down and find them again, without arranging a canvas.

02 · Shelf

Move the same collection below the program. More horizontal room for code, but parked pieces compete with the document’s vertical scroll.

03 · Patches

Separate cards with a little lateral freedom. This sketch staggers cards; it does not implement an infinite canvas. It hints at a larger workspace while exposing the cost of less predictable placement.

Before this becomes live

Bindings travel as metadata, not just spelling. My preferred first version retains source identities and records the free bindings a card needs. Returning it in Refine should require those bindings to resolve as before. Free can explicitly accept a change. An offside card is outside the main evaluation.

Parking is still an edit to the source. Removing a used row or populated expression can change behavior. Refactor and Refine must keep their restrictions; the existence of a shelf does not make removal semantics-preserving.

A small palette first. Arithmetic, functions, matches, literals and a few useful library calls are enough to test the mechanism. A searchable library picker can supply the same kind of template later; it need not become a second permanent sidebar.
